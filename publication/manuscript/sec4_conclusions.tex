In the present work a series of axisymmetric finite-element simulations of cold drawing of AISI~1020 wire was carried out over a factorial grid of process parameters, and the computed residual-stress fields were assembled and filtered into a working dataset of 1756 cases (Section~\ref{sec:fem-dataset}). On this dataset three families of surrogate models were prepared -- a data-driven multilayer perceptron, a strong-form physics-informed network and its weak, variational counterpart -- sharing the architecture, the optimiser, the epoch limit, the early-stopping rule, the mini-batch size and the seeds and differing only in the composition of the loss (Section~\ref{sec:models}). For the comparative assessment several partitions of the preprocessed sample were constructed: a random hold-out, the removal of an interior level of a factor, the removal of the highest level of a single factor, the removal as a whole of a region of the joint space of two factors -- the die semi-angle and the reduction -- left uncovered by the design, the held-out cases of that region still lying inside the training range along each of the two axes taken separately, and random controls of the same size as the corresponding held-out region, which separate the cost of leaving the training range from the cost of a smaller training pool (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Auxiliary series addressed the size of the training pool, the software backend, the two-dimensional formulation with the axial coordinate as an input and the deliberate corruption of the training labels. In total, 341 training runs were performed. On the results of the study the following conclusions can be drawn.

\item On the cleaned dataset of 1756 axisymmetric finite-element simulations, three surrogate families sharing everything but the loss reproduce the reference field to about 8~MPa of macro-RMSE on a random hold-out, with $R^2_{\mathrm{n}} = 0.99$ against 66~MPa for the constant predictor. The cost of prediction outside the training range is governed by the factor withheld rather than by the family: measured against a random control of the same size, the error grows by a factor of 1.08 for the calibration ratio and 1.64 for the die semi-angle, but by 3.15 for the highest reduction and 5.93 for the highest drawing speed, the axis that coincides with the batch of simulations (Table~\ref{tab:axis}).

% src: RESULTS.md §2 / tab:curves caption (slope of Δ on log(fraction): +0.20 MPa, p = 0.21 on random; −0.35 MPa, p = 0.070 on extrap:alpha_max).
\item The three families do not differ in accuracy in a way that this study can establish. On the thirteen splits of the one-dimensional track the strong-form PINN has the lower macro-RMSE, and the difference is significant on four of them in a seed-paired test. However, it amounts to only 0.3--1.9~MPa at an error level of 8--16~MPa, which is comparable with the spread over seeds, and none of the differences from the data-driven baseline remains significant once the Holm correction for the thirteen splits is applied, as Table~\ref{tab:paired} shows. The advantage of the PINN is therefore a consistent tendency rather than a proven gain in accuracy. In the extended track even the tendency is absent: on the random split both forms of the PINN are 0.2~MPa less accurate than the MLP and on the semi-angle extrapolation 0.5~MPa more accurate, and none of these differences is established (Table~\ref{tab:admissibility}). Nor does the physics term help more when the training set is small: as the training set grows, the difference between the PINN and the MLP changes by $+0.20$~MPa per unit of the logarithm of the fraction on the random split and by $-0.35$~MPa on the semi-angle extrapolation, that is, in opposite directions, and neither trend is statistically significant, with $p=0.21$ and $p=0.070$ respectively, as Table~\ref{tab:curves} shows. % NOTE: the accuracy advantage, previously stated as a conclusion, is restated as an observation; no number is replaced.

\item The contribution of the physics term lies in the admissibility of the predicted field and, for the one kind of label defect tested, in a partial protection of the strong form against corrupted training labels, not in accuracy. At an accuracy indistinguishable from that of the data-driven baseline, both forms of the two-dimensional PINN reduce its median radial equilibrium residual by 40--52\,\% and its mean violation of the traction-free condition by a factor of 3.8--6.9 over three seeds, the latter reduction being established for the full form (Table~\ref{tab:admissibility}). Their violation of the boundary condition also falls below that of the finite-element source itself, by a factor of 4.5--6.3 in the single-seed comparison with the source, as does that of the one-dimensional PINN and VPINN on the random split over five seeds (Section~\ref{sec:res_fem}). The reference against which all of the above is measured is the finite-element solution itself. Its adequacy is addressed by the verification described in Section~\ref{sec:res_fem_verification}: on 112 dedicated runs of the same model, covering reductions of 10 and 15\,\%, die semi-angles of 8--16$^\circ$, calibration ratios from 0 to 1, two drawing speeds and all three friction coefficients, the drawing stress obtained from the computed reaction is compared, with no fitted parameters, with the classical analytical descriptions of the process, namely the relation of Siebel and the upper-bound solution of Avitzur, the latter in its friction-factor form and in the Coulomb form on which Vega et~al.\ base their modification. Without their bearing terms the three relations lie above the computed drawing stress in every run, by 11 to 16\,\% on average. They reproduce its dependence on the drawing speed, whereas their sensitivity to friction falls 9 to 25\,\% below the computed one at die semi-angles of $12^\circ$ and $16^\circ$. It is with respect to a reference field verified in this way that the conclusions listed above are to be read. Further work is directed at experimental validation of the residual-stress field. Two independent methods are suited to this. X-ray diffraction measures the stress in a thin surface layer of the drawn wire without destroying it \citep{he2003residual}. The distribution through the thickness requires a separate, destructive method, in which surface layers are removed in succession by etching or machining and the residual stresses are recovered from the deformation of the remaining specimen after each step.
