Physics-informed versus
data-driven surrogates for
residual stress after wire
drawing: accuracy, physical
admissibility and behaviour
outside the training range
Residual stresses left by cold wire drawing govern the fatigue life and dimensional stabil-
ity of the product, yet a finite-element evaluation is too slow for design-space exploration.
We compare data-driven and physics-informed surrogates for the residual stress field of
axisymmetric AISI 1020 drawing on a common dataset of 1756 finite-element simulations,
cleaned from 2443 raw runs by removing friction-label duplicates, an adhered-to-die re-
duction level, and runs that did not complete the draw. Three one-dimensional families—a
data-driven multilayer perceptron (MLP), a strong-form physics-informed neural network
(PINN) and a weak-form variational PINN (VPINN)—share one architecture, optimiser
and training budget and differ only in the loss, so that any difference is attributable to
the physics term. Across thirteen interpolation and extrapolation splits the PINN has a
lower error than the MLP on every split, but the margin is small (0.3–1.9 MPa at an
8–16 MPa error level) and does not survive a multiple-comparison correction; a data-
scarcity advantage is not observed. Two backend implementations (PyTorch and JAX)
are statistically indistinguishable in accuracy, so the cheaper one—here PyTorch, 1.65
times faster—should be preferred. The physics term earns its place elsewhere: it roughly
halves the equilibrium and free-surface residuals of the predicted field, and it makes the
surrogate markedly more robust to corrupted training labels (the PINN–MLP gap grows
by 14.2 MPa per unit corrupted fraction). The finite-element model itself was not experi-
mentally validated; the study therefore characterises the surrogates relative to their data
source.

PINN finite element simulation surrogate modelling
1
Introduction
Metal forming is a fundamental process in modern manufac-
turing, significantly impacting the final geometry, microstructure,
and mechanical properties of manufactured products. Among var-
ious forming operations, wire drawing stands out as a widely em-
ployed technique, yet one that demands exceptional attention to
surface quality, dimensional precision, and performance stability.
The critical factor determining the dimensional stability, fatigue
resistance, and durability of cold-drawn wire lies in the residual
stresses generated during intense and non-uniform plastic defor-
mation processes [1–12].
In particular, as demonstrated in [3]
through precise finite-element analysis, the alternating loading pat-
terns during multi-pass wire drawing result in intricate residual
stress distributions that significantly influence the subsequent be-
havior of the wire. Studies [4,5] indicate that the die geometry and
the conditions of lubrication during the processing of ZnAl15%
alloy significantly affect the magnitude and distribution of residual
stresses. For austenitic stainless steel X5CrNi18-10, as demon-
strated in [10], it has been both experimentally and numerically
verified that adjusting the inlet die angle and drawing speed al-
lows for precise control over the residual stress state. However,

this approach necessitates extensive computational resources. In
the production of superconducting wires based on Nb3Sn, trans-
verse deformations before heat treatment can lead to irreversible
deterioration of critical current [1]. For high-strength, unalloyed
steels like 30MnSi6, ensuring the stability of mechanical proper-
ties after bi-directional drawing is crucial [2]. This imposes strict
limits on the acceptable level of residual stresses, making it a crit-
ical consideration in manufacturing processes. Thus, the capacity
for swift and physically accurate prediction of residual stresses
has become an indispensable aspect of contemporary drawing pro-
cess design. Finite element analysis (FEM), rightfully regarded
as the gold standard for stress-strain modelling during drawing,
allows for highly accurate simulation of stress fields with the aid
of axisymmetric and three-dimensional formulations. These ad-
vanced methods enable the intricate evolution of stress patterns to
be meticulously reproduced, taking into account intricate contact
interactions, substantial plastic deformations, material hardening,
and thermal effects. However, the computational expense associ-
ated with a single FEM analysis remains prohibitively high, ren-
dering it unsuitable for multi-parameter studies, rapid optimization,
and real-time decision-making. This has spurred a surge in interest
in surrogate models that promise orders-of-magnitude reduction in
prediction time while maintaining acceptable levels of accuracy.
The advancements in machine learning (ML) technology have

opened up a whole new realm of possibilities for streamlining finite
element analysis (FEA) in industrial settings [13–15]. By training
artificial neural networks (ANNs) on representative datasets gen-
erated through FEA, these networks can almost instantaneously
approximate highly nonlinear mappings between process parame-
ters and stress fields. In the realm of wire drawing, this approach
has been systematically explored in several studies [16–18]. One
integrated scheme involving both FEA and ANNs [16] showcased
the potential to predict residual stresses with remarkable accuracy.
Moreover, the research conducted in [18] demonstrated that ANNs
are capable of not only predicting residual stresses but also accu-
rately reproducing accumulated strains in wires. Further in-depth
investigations have revealed that when hyperparameters are metic-
ulously optimised using tools such as Optuna [19], BayesianOpt,
or Scikit-optimize, a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) demonstrates
an R² coefficient of determination exceeding 0.99 for all compo-
nents of the residual stress tensor in low-carbon steel wire. Similar
patterns are observed in related domains.
Convolutional neural
networks prove effective in indentation analysis, simultaneously
predicting non-equiaxial residual stresses and plastic flow proper-
ties [20,21]. For real-time estimation of three-dimensional resid-
ual stress distributions during machining, architectures based on
the Incepu-Net approach [22] are employed. Hybrid FEM-ANN-
XGBoost models are utilised for predicting drawing forces [23].
A comprehensive review [15] underscores the prevailing trend to-
wards integrating machine learning techniques for optimising man-
ufacturing processes.
Nonetheless, purely data-driven models have a fundamental
flaw: they cannot guarantee adherence to the laws of physics,
specifically the equations of equilibrium, boundary conditions, and
the governing principles of the mechanics of deformable materi-
als. This issue becomes particularly critical when extending pre-
dictions beyond the scope of the training data, raising legitimate
concerns about the physical validity of the predicted stress pat-
terns. To address this limitation, the concept of Physics-Informed
Neural Networks (PINNs) was introduced. The idea was first pro-
posed in [24] and has undergone rapid development in recent years
[25]. The core concept behind PINNs lies in integrating differential
equations that describe the physical principles of a process directly
into the loss function. This allows the neural network to be penal-
ized not only for discrepancies with the data but also for violating
conservation laws, boundary conditions, and initial conditions. As
demonstrated in [26], this methodology ensures enhanced general-
ization capabilities and physical consistency in predictions for solid
mechanics challenges. A comprehensive analysis [27] highlights
the rapid expansion of physically informed machine learning in a
wide range of manufacturing processes.
There are already successful examples of PINN applications
in predicting residual stresses during ultrasonic surface treatment
of 20CrNiMo steel [28]. Additionally, PINNs have been used to
identify subsurface residual stresses in sliding friction processes
[29,30] and assess durability under high-temperature creep con-
ditions [31]. A comparative analysis of pure neural networks and
physically regularized models applied to sheet metal stamping [32]
demonstrated that PINN-like architectures significantly improve
generalization even with limited training data. The convergence of
hybrid finite-element-machine learning models and physics-driven
machine learning approaches for predicting the properties of addi-
tive manufactured steels underscores a broader trend towards the
integration of prior physical understanding into learning algorithms
[33,34].
Despite the general progress made, the full potential of physical
informed neural networks (PINNs) for predicting residual stresses
after wire drawing remains largely unexplored. In a study [17] that
demonstrated unprecedented accuracy with a purely data-driven
approach, no explicit equilibrium constraints were imposed on the
neural network model. Consequently, it is still not clear whether
physical regularization improves prediction quality, or whether the
rigidity imposed by equilibrium equations reduces accuracy on
real-world datasets. Moreover, the existing literature lacks a sys-
tematic comparison of different PINN implementations, especially
between the strong (differential) and weak (variational) formula-
tions of physical laws for this class of problems. The present study
seeks to bridge this gap by conducting a direct comparative analysis
of six surrogate models, each trained on a unified dataset obtained
through meticulous finite-element simulations of an axisymmetric
wire-drawing process. Two of these models are fundamental data-
centric approaches: a conventional multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
with hyperparameters determined through manual grid search and
an MLP optimized using Optuna, replicating the methodology out-
lined in [17].
The remaining four models represent physically-inspired archi-
tectures: a powerful strong-form physically-informed neural net-
work (PINN) implemented within the PyTorch framework; the
same physical model reproduced using specialized libraries such as
DeepXDE and JAX/Flax, which allows us to evaluate the impact
of software backends on training performance; and a variational
PINN (VPINN) based on the weak (integral) formulation of equi-
librium equations. It is of paramount importance that all six models
employ an identical representation of input features — process pa-
rameters and point coordinates — as well as a uniform structure
for the output vector — components of the residual stress tensor.
Furthermore, a unified approach to clustering data, a consistent
k-fold cross-validation procedure with fixed indices for withheld
observations, and a shared set of quality indicators are employed.
This experimental design guarantees that any statistically sig-
nificant discrepancies in prediction accuracy can be exclusively
attributed to the method of integrating physical knowledge and the
specifics of software implementation, excluding the influence of
extraneous factors. The evaluation is conducted in terms of both
the accuracy in reproducing individual elements of the stress tensor
averaged over the entire field of indicators and their statistical sig-
nificance, which is assessed through the use of non-parametric tests
such as Friedman’s and Neményi’s tests on a dataset comprising
304 testing instances. The findings obtained enable us to furnish
quantitatively supported recommendations regarding the selection
of an appropriate surrogate model architecture for the purpose of
forecasting residual stresses following drawing operations, as well
as delineate the parameters within which the implementation of
rigorous and relaxed forms of physical regularization in the realm
of mechanical engineering processes is feasible.

Materials and methods
2.1
Materials and process parameters. The process mod-
elled is axisymmetric cold drawing of a wire through a conical
die. The computational domain and the notation used for its bound-
aries are shown in Fig. 1(a). Five characteristic surfaces are dis-
tinguished: 𝑆1 - is the axis of symmetry of the billet and of the
finished product; 𝑆2 - is the outlet surface, on which the drawing
velocity is prescribed; 𝑆3 - is the free lateral surface; 𝑆4 - is the
contact surface between the wire and the die; and 𝑆5 - is the entry
surface of the billet.
The half-angle of the working cone of the die is denoted 𝛼;
𝐷0 and 𝐷are the diameters of the billet and of the drawn wire;
𝐿is the length of the calibration land. The radial coordinate 𝑟
is measured from the axis and the axial coordinate 𝑧along the
direction of metal flow. The three geometric parameters of Table 1
are expressed through this notation: the reduction 𝑄= (𝑟0 −𝑟1)/𝑟0
follows from 𝐷0 and 𝐷, the deformation-zone half-angle is 𝛼, and
the calibration ratio 𝑘relates the land length 𝐿to the outlet radius.
A series of axisymmetric FEM simulations of the wire-drawing
process was carried out in Abaqus CAE. The workpiece was a rect-
angular longitudinal section discretised with a CAX4RT element
mesh. The geometry of the die was described by three parameters:
the cross-section reduction (𝑄), the half-angle of the deformation
zone (𝛼), and the ratio between the calibration-section length and
the outlet radius (𝑘). Process parameters varied during the cam-
paign were the Coulomb friction coefficient (𝜇) and the drawing
velocity (𝑣). Their ranges are summarised in Table 1.

Material behaviour was specified with a Johnson–Cook constitu-
tive model for AISI 1020 [35,36]. As recovered from the exported
stress fields, however, the response over the range covered is effec-
tively elastic–perfectly-plastic with a yield stress of 320 ± 10 MPa,
with no detectable strain-rate or temperature sensitivity, consis-
tent with an analysis that is not thermomechanically coupled; the
Johnson–Cook hardening and softening constants therefore do not
act on the exported result and are not used in the surrogate models.
The same constitutive description was used in all simulations, so
the resulting dataset reflects pure variation of geometry and pro-
cess conditions. Spatial coordinates of mesh nodes and the four
cylindrical components of the residual stress tensor were exported
from the Abaqus Output Database (.odb) files through scripts writ-
ten against the Abaqus CAE Python API. Only the nodes lying
within 25–75% of the wire cross-section height were retained for
surrogate-model training, because this region corresponds to the
steady-state deformation regime and is most representative of the
residual stress field of interest. Each retained set was downsampled
to 20 radial points so that all simulations share the same spatial
grid.

In this study, no direct experimental validation of the finite el-
ement model was carried out (i.e. no measurement of the drag
force or residual stresses using X-ray diffraction). The modelling
approach employed in this study aligns with established practices
in the numerical analysis of wire drawing. This approach encom-
passes the utilisation of finite elements, a contact formulation, and a
material description based on the Johnson–Cook model. This prac-
tice, as demonstrated in previous independent studies [3,6,7,10], is
in good agreement with experimental measurements, which serves
as indirect confirmation of the model’s reliability. Nevertheless, it
should be noted that such an agreement does not constitute direct
validation of the specific dataset obtained here. Consequently, it is
imperative that the resulting residual stress values are interpreted
with this limitation in mind.

Filtering.. Cases are removed whole, before any split, in the
order of Table 3. The level 𝑄= 0.25 is excluded entirely: there
the process ceased to be drawing in several runs (the billet adhered
to the die and the step degenerated into tension), and 17.5 % of
its runs did not complete against 4.3–6.0 % at the other levels.
One run with a misspelled friction token is dropped. Cases whose
profile is bit-identical to another case differing only in the friction
label are dropped, keeping one per group (323 of the 2443 raw
cases, 13.2 %; 319 of them survive the two preceding filters and
are counted in Table 3). Finally, runs that stopped before the wire
had passed through the die are removed: their tail retains the billet
radius 𝑅0 and the die reference point lies inside the axial extent of
the wire (153 of the 159 such runs on the raw grid). Where the
undrawn part enters the window the surface radius varies along it

the profile labels as free-surface 𝜎𝑟𝑟is contact pressure, down to
−571.3 MPa against −19.7 MPa at worst in clean cases. These
92 cases (89 detected through the window plus 3 with the tail
outside it) are removed before splitting: on the random split of the
uncleaned set 73 of the 89 fell into the training pool, where the
data term contradicts the traction-free condition.
The final dataset has 1756 cases: 35 120 radial points in the
main track and 280 960 grid points in the extended track.
Group-aware splits.. All partitions are made at the level of
cases, so the 20 radial points (or 160 grid points) of a case
never straddle training and test. A random 15 % hold-out mea-
sures interpolation only; to measure transfer beyond the training
range, entire factor levels are held out (Table 4).
Each extrap-
olation split has two controls:
a matched control holds out a
random subset of the same size from the whole pool, so that
ΔRMSE = RMSE(extrap) −RMSE(matched) separates the cost
of leaving the training range from that of a smaller training set; an
interior control holds out an inner level of the same axis (𝛼= 12◦,
𝑄= 0.10), separating the cost of a gap in the factor grid from
that of leaving its range. The corner split removes the joint re-
gion 𝛼= 20◦∧𝑄≥0.15; along each axis separately its test cases
stay inside the training range, so it probes an uncovered region of
the joint space, not extrapolation. A coverage report lists per axis
whether the test range leaves the training range; transfer beyond
the training range is claimed only for axes where it does.
The

Training protocol.. The protocol is enforced by construction
rather than by configuration: every family is trained by the same
trainer class, in which the family selects only the composition of the
loss, and one launcher writes every run with its hyperparameters to
a single log. It is identical for all families: AdamW, learning rate
3×10−3 (2×10−3 in the extended track), no schedule, weight decay
10−4, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, mini-batches of 64 cases (32),
at most 800 epochs (300), early stopping on the macro-RMSE of
an internal early-stopping subset of 15 % of the training cases with
patience 40 (30), and restoration of the best state. One architecture
and one hyperparameter set were fixed before any held-out region
was evaluated and were not tuned per family or per split, so no
search could leak a held-out region. Every configuration is trained
with five seeds (0–4) on the thirteen splits of Table 4 and in the
backend comparison, and with three seeds (0–2) in the learning-
curve, label-corruption and extended-track stages; the seed governs
initialisation, batch order and the internal early-stopping subset,
while the test cases of a split are the same for every seed (split
seed 42). Learning curves use nested group-aware subsamples of
10/25/50/100 % of the training pool, identical for all families; the
subsamples are nested but not stratified by 𝑣(the launcher uses
nested_subsamples, not the stratified variant), so the sparsest
level 𝑣= 5 m/min (122 cases) can be under-represented at 10 %;
label corruption adds +250 MPa to 𝜎𝜃𝜃of 0/1/5/10 % of the train-
ing cases (0 % being the clean reference; nested lists shared by all
families, hold-out clean), the morphology of a solver artefact found
in the source rather than Gaussian noise, which averages out over
a profile.

The ablation in Table 9 isolates the axial equation; its residuals
are evaluated by the training code in MPa/m on the 1756-case splits
and are not numerically comparable to the dimensionless medians
of Table 8. Both physics-informed forms reduce the radial resid-
ual by 40–52 %, since both contain the radial equation. The axial
residual falls by 33–41 % with the full form and by only 9–14 %
with the reduced one, which drops 𝜕𝜎𝑧𝑧/𝜕𝑧, a term amounting to
68 % of the retained ones, and therefore cannot enforce axial bal-
ance. The gain in axial equilibrium thus comes from the axial term
in the loss, not from “physics in general”, and costs no accuracy:
the macro-RMSE changes by +0.2 MPa on the random split and
−0.5 MPa on the extrapolation, within the seed spread.
The corruption experiment (Table 13) yields the strongest and
most interpretable effect of the physics term.
On clean labels
the PINN and the MLP are indistinguishable (Δ = −0.10 MPa,
𝑝= 0.72): the term adds nothing where data and equations al-
ready agree. As labels are corrupted the gap opens monotonically,
−0.10 →−0.48 →−1.11 →−1.59 MPa, and is established at
5 and 10 %. A regression of the gap on the corrupted fraction
over the twelve seed-level points gives −14.2 MPa per unit fraction
(𝑝= 0.00036, 𝑅2 = 0.74): the advantage grows by about 1.4 MPa
for every 10 % of corrupted labels. At 10 % the MLP has degraded
by a factor of 1.81 and the PINN by 1.64; the weak form (1.79) is
not protected in the same way. The mechanism is the one seen in
Table 8: the strong-form residual and the traction-free term pull the
solution towards an admissible field, and the pull matters exactly
when the labels contradict it. Taken together, Tables 8 and 13 lo-
cate the benefit of the physics term not in accuracy against a clean
finite-element field, where it is marginal, but in the admissibility
of the solution and its resistance to defects of the data source.
Limitations.. The surrogates are trained and evaluated against
a single finite-element model that has not been validated against
physical measurements (Section 2.2). This study therefore does
not assess how accurately any of the models represents the wire-
drawing process; it assesses properties of the surrogates relative
to their data source, transferability beyond the training range and
robustness to defects of that source, which are measurable with-
out experimental validation and are what is claimed. The facto-
rial grid is incomplete and unbalanced: the level 𝑣= 5 m/min
is represented by 122 cases against 554–607 for the other levels,
speed coincides with the simulation batch, and 13.2 % of the raw
cases are bit-identical duplicates of another case differing only in
the friction label, so friction cannot be used as an extrapolation
axis. The shear component 𝜏𝑟𝑧is essentially not predicted from
the one-dimensional representation: its NRMSE (held-out standard
deviation as denominator) is 0.47–0.49 on the random hold-out and
1.6–1.9 with the highest speed withheld, against a physical scale
of 0.67 MPa mean absolute value, two orders of magnitude be-
low the normal components; macro-averaged figures are therefore
dominated by the normal components, and per-component figures
are given alongside them.
4
Conclusions
(1) On a cleaned dataset of 1756 axisymmetric finite-element
simulations, three surrogate families sharing everything but
the loss reach about 8 MPa macro-RMSE on a random hold-
out with 𝑅2n = 0.99. The strong-form PINN is at least as
accurate as the data-driven MLP on all thirteen interpolation
and extrapolation splits, but the advantage (0.3–1.9 MPa) is
small and does not survive a Holm correction for multiple
comparisons.

(2) A benefit of the physics prior specifically under data scarcity
was not observed: the learning-curve trends are not statis-
tically significant and are of opposite sign on the two splits
examined.
(3) The two software backends, PyTorch and JAX, are statisti-
cally indistinguishable in accuracy and differ only in cost,
PyTorch being 1.65 times faster on CPU in double precision.
Backend choice is therefore a matter of computational cost,
not of result; the earlier conclusion that the backend affects
accuracy is withdrawn.
(4) The clearest value of the physics term is physical admissibil-
ity. At equal accuracy the strong-form and full-form PINNs
reduce the dimensionless radial and axial equilibrium resid-
uals and the traction-free violation of the predicted field well
below the MLP, and below the finite-element source itself
for the boundary condition.
(5) The physics term makes the surrogate robust to defects of the
data source: as training labels are corrupted the PINN–MLP
accuracy gap grows monotonically, by 14.2 MPa per unit
corrupted fraction (𝑝= 0.0004), whereas on clean labels
the two are indistinguishable.
(6) The shear component 𝜏𝑟𝑧cannot be recovered from a one-
dimensional, window-averaged representation, because 79 %
of its in-window variance lies along the axial coordinate
and cancels under averaging; a two-dimensional formulation
with 𝑧as an input predicts it with 𝑅2 = 0.94.
(7) The finite-element model was not validated against physical
measurements. These conclusions therefore concern the sur-
rogates relative to their data source—accuracy on the finite-
element field, transferability beyond the training range, and
robustness to defects of that source—and not the accuracy
of the drawing simulation itself.
